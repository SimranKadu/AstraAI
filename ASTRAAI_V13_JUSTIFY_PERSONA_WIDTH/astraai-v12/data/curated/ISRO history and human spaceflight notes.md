# ISRO history and human spaceflight notes

## Founding of India's space programme

India's space programme began in the early 1960s under Dr. Vikram Sarabhai, who believed that space technology should serve national development through communication, education, weather forecasting and resource mapping. In 1962 the government set up the Indian National Committee for Space Research (INCOSPAR) with Sarabhai as its chairman. The Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) was formed on 15 August 1969, and the Department of Space and the Space Commission were created in 1972.

## How the programme began with limited resources

The early programme had very little money, equipment or infrastructure, so it grew through small, inventive steps. The Thumba Equatorial Rocket Launching Station near Thiruvananthapuram was chosen in 1963 because the magnetic equator passes close by, which suits upper-atmosphere research. Its first offices and workshops were set up in a local church, the bishop's house and a cattle shed, and early rocket parts were carried by bicycle and bullock cart. The first sounding rocket was launched from Thumba on 21 November 1963. Young engineers were recruited and trained on the job, and the team built its own rockets and instruments step by step, starting with sounding rockets before moving on to satellite launch vehicles.

## Early satellites and space for development

India's first satellite, Aryabhata, was built by ISRO and launched on 19 April 1975 on a Soviet rocket. Sarabhai had died in December 1971 and did not see it. The Satellite Instructional Television Experiment (SITE) of 1975 and 1976 used a borrowed NASA satellite to broadcast educational television to villages in several states, showing how space could reach rural communities. The Rohini, Bhaskara and APPLE satellites followed in the late 1970s and early 1980s, and the INSAT series, launched from 1982, built the national communication and weather network.

## SLV-3, India's first satellite launch vehicle

SLV-3 (Satellite Launch Vehicle-3) was India's first experimental launch vehicle for satellites. It was a four-stage, all solid-propellant rocket about 22 metres tall, weighing about 17 tonnes at liftoff and able to place a payload of roughly 40 kg in low Earth orbit. Dr. A. P. J. Abdul Kalam was the project director. It flew four times from Sriharikota. The first launch on 10 August 1979 failed. The second launch on 18 July 1980 placed the 35 kg Rohini RS-1 satellite in orbit. The third launch in 1981 reached a lower orbit than intended, and the fourth launch in 1983 succeeded. The 1980 success made India one of the few countries able to launch a satellite on its own rocket.

## Engineering lessons commonly drawn from SLV-3

The control and guidance system must be tested exhaustively. The 1979 failure was traced to a fault in the second-stage control system and was studied in detail before the next launch.

Failure analysis should be open and systematic. Data from every flight, including the failed one, drove design changes for the 1980 flight.

Redundancy and strict quality checks matter, because one small valve or component failure can lose an entire vehicle.

Building solid-propellant motors, stage separation, heat protection and closed-loop guidance in-house created the skills that later made the ASLV and PSLV possible. The SLV-3 first stage was also used in India's early Agni technology demonstrator.

Integrating many specialist groups under one mission goal is a leadership skill in itself. As recounted in Kalam's memoir Wings of Fire, ISRO chairman Satish Dhawan publicly took responsibility for the 1979 failure and later let the project team face the press after the 1980 success.

## Learning from a launch failure

Treat a failure as data, not as blame. Good practice drawn from ISRO's experience is to take responsibility as a leader, analyse the failure openly with the whole team, find and fix the root cause, test again before flying, and credit the team when things succeed. SLV-3 failed on its first flight in 1979 and succeeded in 1980. The first PSLV launch on 20 September 1993 did not reach orbit because of a control problem, and PSLV flew successfully in October 1994 and has since become a very reliable launcher. The Vikram lander of Chandrayaan-2 was lost during the final descent in September 2019. Chandrayaan-3 was then redesigned using a failure-based approach with sturdier landing sensors, more fuel and a larger landing area, and it landed successfully on 23 August 2023.

## Prof. Satish Dhawan

Satish Dhawan (1920 to 2002) was an aerospace engineer known for his research on boundary layers in fluid flow. He directed the Indian Institute of Science in Bengaluru from 1962 to 1981 and chaired ISRO from 1972 to 1984. He is remembered for a leadership style that protected young engineers, accepted responsibility for failures and shared credit for successes. The Sriharikota launch centre was renamed the Satish Dhawan Space Centre after his death.

## Dr. A. P. J. Abdul Kalam

A. P. J. Abdul Kalam (1931 to 2015) was born in Rameswaram, Tamil Nadu. He worked at ISRO, where he was project director of SLV-3, and later at DRDO on missile programmes including Agni and Prithvi. He served as Principal Scientific Adviser to the Government of India and as the 11th President of India from 2002 to 2007. He is known as the Missile Man of India and wrote the memoir Wings of Fire.

## Dr. Vikram Sarabhai

Vikram Sarabhai (1919 to 1971) was born in Ahmedabad and was a physicist who studied cosmic rays. He founded the Physical Research Laboratory in 1947, led INCOSPAR from 1962, and is regarded as the father of the Indian space programme. He championed the use of satellites for education, communication and development. The Vikram lander of Chandrayaan-2 and Chandrayaan-3 is named after him.

## Rakesh Sharma and India's first astronaut flight

Rakesh Sharma, an Indian Air Force pilot, became the first Indian to travel to space. He flew on the Soviet Soyuz T-11 spacecraft, launched on 3 April 1984, and spent about eight days in orbit, working aboard the Salyut 7 space station on science and Earth-observation experiments. When Prime Minister Indira Gandhi asked how India looked from space, he replied that it looked better than the rest of the world, echoing the song Saare Jahan Se Accha.

## Gaganyaan, India's human spaceflight programme

The goal of Gaganyaan is to demonstrate India's ability to send a crew of up to three astronauts to low Earth orbit at about 400 km for up to three days and bring them home safely, landing in the sea near the Indian coast. It uses a human-rated version of the LVM3 rocket and an orbital module made of a crew module and a service module, with a crew escape system for safety. ISRO is building up to the crewed flight through uncrewed test flights, including one carrying the robotic humanoid Vyommitra. The Test Vehicle Abort Mission TV-D1 on 21 October 2023 demonstrated the crew escape system and recovery from the Bay of Bengal. Four Indian Air Force test pilots were named astronaut-designates in February 2024 and trained in Russia and at the Astronaut Training Facility in Bengaluru. One of them, Group Captain Shubhanshu Shukla, flew to the International Space Station on the private Axiom-4 mission in mid-2025. The date of the first crewed Gaganyaan flight has been revised several times, so check ISRO for the latest schedule. India has also announced longer-term goals of a national space station and a crewed Moon landing.

## Chandrayaan-3: how it reached the Moon

Chandrayaan-3 launched on 14 July 2023 on an LVM3-M4 rocket from Sriharikota into an elliptical Earth orbit. Over the following weeks the propulsion module fired its engine several times to raise the orbit. On 1 August 2023 a trans-lunar injection burn sent it toward the Moon, and on 5 August 2023 it was captured by lunar gravity. Further burns lowered it to a circular orbit about 100 km above the Moon. The lander module, Vikram carrying the Pragyan rover, separated on 17 August 2023 and lowered its orbit to about 30 km by 100 km. Powered descent began from about 30 km altitude on 23 August 2023, and Vikram touched down near the lunar south pole at about 18:04 IST. Pragyan rolled out and worked for about one lunar day, roughly 14 Earth days. The landing site was named Shiv Shakti Point, and 23 August is now celebrated as National Space Day.

## Chandrayaan-3 science results

Vikram carried instruments for lunar soil temperature (ChaSTE), plasma near the surface (RAMBHA-LP) and seismic activity (ILSA), plus a laser retroreflector. The Pragyan rover carried a laser-induced breakdown spectroscope and an alpha particle X-ray spectrometer. They confirmed sulphur and other elements in the lunar soil near the south pole, and ChaSTE recorded a steep temperature drop within the top few centimetres of soil.

## Kalpana Chawla

Kalpana Chawla (1962 to 2003) was born in Karnal, Haryana, studied aerospace engineering and became a NASA astronaut. She was the first woman of Indian origin in space, flying on Space Shuttle Columbia mission STS-87 in November 1997 as a mission specialist and robotic arm operator. She died with her six crewmates when Columbia broke up on re-entry at the end of mission STS-107 on 1 February 2003. ISRO's Kalpana-1 weather satellite was named in her honour.

## Sunita Williams

Sunita Williams was born in 1965 in Euclid, Ohio, to an Indian-American father. She is a retired US Navy captain and helicopter pilot who was selected as a NASA astronaut in 1998. She flew long missions to the International Space Station in 2006 to 2007 and 2012, commanding the station during Expedition 33. In June 2024 she launched on the first crewed flight of Boeing's Starliner for what was planned as a stay of about a week. Because of thruster and helium problems the capsule returned uncrewed, and she and Butch Wilmore stayed aboard for about nine months, coming home on a SpaceX Dragon in March 2025. She has completed nine spacewalks, holds the record for most spacewalking time by a woman, and has spent more than 600 days in space.

## Daily life and teamwork aboard the International Space Station

The International Space Station orbits about 400 km above Earth at roughly 7.7 km per second, circling the planet about every 90 minutes, so the crew sees around 16 sunrises and sunsets a day. A typical crew is seven people from NASA, Roscosmos, ESA, JAXA and the Canadian Space Agency, and the station runs on UTC. A normal day includes science experiments, maintenance and repairs, conferences with ground teams, and about two hours of exercise on a treadmill, bike and resistance machine to protect muscles and bones in weightlessness. Astronauts sleep in small sleeping bags fixed to a wall and eat mostly packaged and rehydrated food, using tortillas instead of bread to avoid crumbs. Water is recycled, including from sweat and urine. Crews rely on clear communication, shared procedures and trust, because everyone depends on each other for repairs, spacewalks and emergencies. Meals together and video calls with family help keep morale high.
